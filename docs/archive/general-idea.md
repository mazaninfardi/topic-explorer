With the advances in technology, we are seeing more needs for learning new topics at a faster pace and have to choose what we want to learn and how deep we would like to learn something. When learning a new topic we have to learn several things. If we use an academic paper example , we need to understand the scope, what the background was, what the the gap was, how the paper addresses the issue, and how they achieved it. Not all of this is necessary to get the gist of the paper, you only need to now what they did and what is their achievement. If that is more interesting, then you’d ask why this was needed. You might also ask how they are going to do this, only then. While exploring what/why/how, you might be also interested in learning more details about each step, and you might also encounter new terms that you are unfmailar with.

Before the LLM era, people were taking notes on the PDF, or a print out, trying to build a mental model of the whole context, and keep many competing topics in their short memory. With the LLMs, we can simplify the process, by asking for a summary, a definition for any item that we don’t understand, and rely again on our short-term memory for putting the pieces of the puzzle together. However, as we know, short-term memory has a limited capacity. But as George Armitage Miller's 1956 paper says in "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two," the average human short-term memory capacity is limited to about seven items +/- 2.

How do we solve it? We cannot scale ourselves, so we need to make sure that we go deeper into a topic and oncer we have a good understanding focus on the higher level definition, e.g., if a paper mentioned neural network, and the reader is not familiar with that, we want them to focus on the topic without losing the high level content.

Topic explorer is a way to achieve this in a visual way, as we will see it, it helps you keep your focus on the core concepts and keep them below 7 +/- 2
